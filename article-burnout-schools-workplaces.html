Burnout in Schools and Workplaces: Why Connection Matters More Than Ever

Burnout is often described as the result of doing too much for too long. While workload, pressure, and expectations certainly contribute to exhaustion, burnout is rarely caused by one factor alone. By Corinne Siaosi, Social Health Systems Intern

5 min read

Burnout is often described as the result of doing too much for too long. While workload, pressure, and expectations certainly contribute to exhaustion, burnout is rarely caused by one factor alone.

The way people experience their environment plays a significant role.

A demanding workplace can feel manageable when individuals have strong support, meaningful relationships, and a sense that they are part of a connected community. The same responsibilities can feel overwhelming when people feel isolated, misunderstood, or disconnected from those around them.

This distinction is important because many approaches to burnout focus primarily on individual solutions. People are encouraged to improve time management, take breaks, practice self-care, or develop better coping strategies. These tools can be valuable, but they do not address the larger conditions that influence whether people feel supported or depleted.

Burnout is not only about how much people are carrying. It is also about the environment in which they are carrying it.

In schools and workplaces, relational health plays an important role in preventing chronic stress. When people feel connected, respected, and supported, they are better equipped to navigate challenges. When connection breaks down, everyday pressures become significantly harder to manage.

The Hidden Factors Behind Burnout

Traditional conversations about burnout often focus on workload.

A teacher may be overwhelmed by responsibilities. An employee may be struggling with deadlines and expectations. A student may feel pressure from academics, activities, and social demands.

However, workload alone does not explain why some people remain resilient under pressure while others experience exhaustion.

The difference often comes from the surrounding environment.

People are affected by whether they feel supported by the people around them. They are affected by whether they can ask for help, whether their concerns are taken seriously, and whether they feel their efforts are recognized.

When individuals experience constant pressure without meaningful connection, stress becomes more difficult to recover from.

A person who feels alone while facing challenges carries a different emotional burden than someone who knows they have support.

This is why burnout should be viewed as more than a personal struggle. It is also influenced by the culture, communication patterns, and relationships within an environment.

How Disconnection Contributes to Exhaustion

Human beings are not designed to navigate ongoing stress completely on their own.

Supportive relationships help people process challenges, maintain perspective, and recover from difficult experiences. When those relationships are missing, stress can become heavier and more difficult to manage.

In workplaces, disconnection may appear when employees feel separated from their teams, hesitant to communicate concerns, or disconnected from the purpose behind their work.

A person may continue meeting expectations while quietly losing motivation and energy.

In schools, students may experience burnout when academic demands combine with social stress and a lack of connection. A student who feels unsupported may struggle more with challenges that another student can manage with the right relationships around them.

Disconnection does not always look obvious. Many people experiencing burnout continue showing up and completing responsibilities.

The issue is that they may no longer feel engaged, energized, or connected to what they are doing.

Burnout in the Workplace

Workplace burnout has become a significant concern across industries because modern work often requires constant communication, adaptation, and problem-solving.

Employees are expected to collaborate effectively while managing increasing demands and changing expectations.

When workplace culture does not provide adequate support, these pressures can accumulate.

Employees may experience burnout when they feel that they cannot be honest about challenges, when communication feels purely transactional, or when their contributions go unnoticed.

Over time, this can lead to emotional exhaustion and disengagement.

A healthy workplace does not eliminate stress. Every organization has periods of pressure and uncertainty. The difference is whether employees have the relationships and systems needed to navigate those moments.

Strong workplace cultures create environments where people can ask questions, communicate concerns, and work through challenges without feeling isolated.

Connection does not remove responsibility. It makes responsibility more sustainable.

Burnout in Schools

Schools are also environments where burnout can develop.

Students are balancing academic expectations, social relationships, extracurricular commitments, and personal challenges. Educators are managing instruction, classroom needs, administrative responsibilities, and the emotional demands of supporting students.

When these pressures occur without strong relational support, burnout becomes more likely.

For students, burnout may appear as loss of motivation, withdrawal, frustration, or difficulty engaging with learning.

For educators, burnout may appear as emotional exhaustion, reduced enthusiasm, and feeling disconnected from the purpose that originally brought them into education.

Addressing burnout in schools requires more than focusing on academic performance or workload management. It requires creating environments where students and staff feel supported by the people around them.

Relationships are a protective factor.

When people feel connected, they are better prepared to handle challenges.

Why Wellness Programs Often Fall Short

Many organizations recognize burnout as a serious issue and respond by introducing wellness initiatives.

These efforts may include additional resources, mental health benefits, flexibility programs, or opportunities for relaxation.

These supports can be helpful, but they often focus on helping individuals manage stress after it has already developed.

A deeper approach considers what creates the conditions where burnout grows.

If an employee feels disconnected from their team, an occasional wellness activity will not repair that relationship.

If a student feels isolated from their peers, a single presentation about stress management will not create a sense of belonging.

Sustainable change requires attention to the everyday experiences that shape how people interact with one another.

How people communicate.

How leaders respond.

How teams handle challenges.

How individuals feel within the community around them.

These factors influence whether people experience an environment as supportive or exhausting.

Building Resilience Through Stronger Connections

Resilience is often discussed as an individual trait, but environments also influence resilience.

People are more capable of handling challenges when they have strong relationships and reliable support systems.

Building healthier cultures requires intentional attention to the behaviors that strengthen connection.

This includes creating opportunities for meaningful communication, encouraging people to seek support, recognizing contributions, and developing stronger interpersonal skills.

When relationships improve, many of the conditions that contribute to burnout begin to change.

People are more willing to communicate challenges before they become overwhelming.

Teams are better able to solve problems together.

Individuals feel less like they are carrying pressure alone.

Resilience grows when people know they are supported.

Moving Toward Healthier Organizational Cultures

Burnout is not solved by simply asking people to work harder on their own well-being. Sustainable change requires understanding the environment surrounding them.

Schools and workplaces are communities, and communities influence how people experience stress, connection, and purpose.

When relational health becomes a priority, organizations create stronger foundations for long-term success. People are better able to collaborate, communicate, and remain engaged when they feel connected to the people around them.

Social Health Systems helps schools and workplaces evaluate the relational factors that influence communication, culture, and overall well-being. Organizations interested in creating stronger standards for social health can learn more about the Social Health Accreditation (SHa) process.

Burnout may begin with exhaustion, but recovery and prevention often begin with connection. Creating environments where people feel supported is one of the most effective ways to build healthier, more sustainable communities.
