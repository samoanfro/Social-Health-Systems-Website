Loneliness in the Workplace and Schools: Why Social Connection Has Become an Organizational Priority

Loneliness is often associated with being physically alone. However, some of the most significant experiences of loneliness happen when people are surrounded by others. By Corinne Siaosi, Social Health Systems Intern

5 min read

Loneliness is often associated with being physically alone. However, some of the most significant experiences of loneliness happen when people are surrounded by others.

A student can sit in a classroom full of classmates and still feel invisible. An employee can spend an entire day in meetings, communicate with coworkers, and contribute to projects while feeling disconnected from the people around them.

This is the overlooked reality of loneliness in modern organizations. It is not always obvious. It does not always appear as isolation. Instead, it often shows up through disengagement, reduced participation, emotional withdrawal, and a growing sense that people are simply moving through their day without meaningful connection.

Schools and workplaces have invested significant resources into mental health programs, wellness initiatives, and support systems. These efforts matter, but many organizations continue to struggle with a deeper challenge: people do not always feel connected to the communities they are part of.

Loneliness has become a cultural issue.

When individuals feel disconnected from their environment, it affects how they communicate, collaborate, and participate. Over time, loneliness can weaken the relationships that allow organizations to function effectively.

Addressing loneliness requires more than encouraging people to socialize. It requires understanding how environments create opportunities for connection, belonging, and shared purpose.

The Hidden Nature of Loneliness

One reason loneliness is difficult for organizations to address is that it is often invisible.

Someone experiencing loneliness may still attend meetings, complete assignments, meet expectations, and appear successful from the outside. Unlike a physical injury or visible challenge, loneliness often happens internally.

This makes it easy to overlook.

In workplaces, loneliness may appear as an employee who stops contributing ideas, avoids collaboration, or feels disconnected from the mission of the organization. They may continue performing their responsibilities while becoming less engaged with the people around them.

In schools, loneliness may appear through students withdrawing from peers, avoiding participation, struggling to form friendships, or feeling like they do not belong within the school community.

These behaviors are sometimes misunderstood as a lack of motivation or effort.

However, connection influences how people experience their environment. When people feel like they are not truly part of a group, they often become less likely to participate fully.

Loneliness does not only affect individuals. It affects the culture around them.

Why Modern Environments Increase Disconnection

The way people work and learn has changed dramatically.

Employees may spend more time communicating digitally than interacting face-to-face. Hybrid workplaces can provide flexibility while also reducing everyday opportunities for informal conversations and relationship building.

Students navigate increasingly complex social environments while balancing academic demands, digital communication, and changing expectations around connection.

These changes do not automatically create loneliness, but they can make it easier for people to feel disconnected.

Many environments are designed around efficiency.

Schedules are optimized. Tasks are organized. Information moves quickly.

But connection often depends on something different.

People build relationships through repeated experiences of recognition, interaction, and shared moments. When those opportunities disappear, loneliness can grow even in highly productive environments.

Organizations often focus on what people accomplish but overlook how connected people feel while accomplishing it.

The Organizational Cost of Loneliness

Loneliness is not only a personal experience. It has measurable effects on organizations.

When people feel disconnected, collaboration often becomes more difficult. Individuals may be less likely to ask for help, share ideas, or engage in conversations that require vulnerability.

In workplaces, this can contribute to:

Reduced teamwork.

Lower engagement.

Increased employee turnover.

Weaker communication between departments.

In schools, loneliness can contribute to:

Reduced classroom participation.

Difficulty forming peer relationships.

Lower engagement with learning.

Increased social withdrawal.

A connected environment does not mean everyone becomes close friends. Healthy organizations still need professionalism, boundaries, and individual differences.

The goal is not constant interaction.

The goal is creating environments where people feel that they are part of something meaningful.

Why Traditional Solutions Often Miss the Root Cause

Many organizations attempt to address loneliness through occasional activities.

Team lunches, social events, and employee appreciation programs can create positive experiences. However, they often do not address the everyday conditions that influence whether people feel connected.

Loneliness is not usually created by one missing event.

It develops through repeated experiences.

A person begins to feel disconnected when interactions consistently feel transactional, when they do not feel recognized, or when they lack meaningful opportunities to contribute.

This means organizations need to think beyond adding more events and focus on strengthening the culture itself.

Connection is built through everyday experiences, not isolated moments.

Building Environments Where People Feel Connected

Reducing loneliness requires organizations to examine how people experience their environment.

Do employees feel comfortable asking for support?

Do students feel recognized by their peers and educators?

Do people have opportunities to contribute beyond simply completing tasks?

These questions reveal whether an organization is creating conditions where connection can grow.

Social Health Systems focuses on helping organizations understand and strengthen the human factors that influence culture, communication, and belonging. The Social Health Systems framework provides additional insight into how organizations can evaluate and improve the social conditions that affect people every day.

The goal is not to force relationships.

It is to create environments where relationships have room to develop.

Supporting Connection in Schools and Workplaces

Schools and workplaces experience loneliness differently, but the underlying need is the same: people want to feel that they matter within the communities they are part of.

For schools, addressing loneliness means creating environments where students have opportunities to develop relationships, participate confidently, and feel supported by the people around them.

For workplaces, it means creating cultures where employees feel connected to their teams, their leaders, and the purpose behind their work.

Connection should not be viewed as an extra benefit.

It is part of how organizations function.

People who feel connected are more likely to participate, collaborate, and contribute to shared goals.

Measuring and Strengthening Social Health

Organizations often measure performance, attendance, productivity, and outcomes. Fewer organizations measure the quality of the relationships that support those outcomes.

This is changing.

As loneliness becomes a larger concern across workplaces and schools, organizations are recognizing that social health deserves intentional attention.

Through Social Health Accreditation (SHa), organizations can establish clearer standards for evaluating and strengthening the social conditions that influence culture.

A healthier environment does not happen by accident.

It requires awareness, consistency, and a commitment to understanding how people experience the communities around them.

Creating a Future With Stronger Connection

Loneliness does not disappear simply because people share the same space.

A crowded workplace can still feel isolating. A busy school can still leave students feeling disconnected.

The solution is not creating more opportunities for people to be around one another. It is creating environments where people experience meaningful interaction, support, and belonging.

Organizations that prioritize social health create stronger communities where people are more willing to participate and contribute.

Loneliness is a human experience, but addressing it requires an organizational response.

When schools and workplaces intentionally build cultures of connection, they create environments where people can do more than simply show up.

They can belong.
